## Chapter 1

## Chapter 1: Cinderella

## Scene 1

The servants' wing smelled of lye and old stone.

Leah knelt at the stone trough with her sleeves rolled past the elbows, the raw skin of her hands already burning where the water bit into the small cuts along her knuckles. Eight years of this. Eight years of lye, of cold that settled in the marrow, of rising before the sun and sleeping after the last footstep faded. The castle above them wore velvet and candlelight; down here the walls sweated moisture and the floors stayed cold even in summer. Leah was twenty. She had been twelve when the fever came.

She kept her head down. Not from humility. From habit.

The trough was older than she was, worn smooth by generations of hands that had scrubbed other people's linen. The water in it was gray by the time she finished, thick with the dirt of people who did not know they were being cleaned. She emptied it twice a day, carried it to the drain in the yard, hauled it back from the well. Her shoulders ached. Her back ached. Her hands ached in a way that had stopped being pain and become simply weather, the way a tree does not feel the wind that bends it.

She knew the other servants by their sounds rather than their faces. There was the woman who coughed in the third stall. There was the boy who hummed when he thought no one was listening. There was the old man who had been here before Leah was born and who moved now like a man underwater, slow and patient and already half gone. They did not speak to her. She was the king's daughter, and she was not one of them, and everyone knew it, and no one said it. She scrubbed. They scrubbed. The lye did not care who held the brush.

She worked in the rhythm she had built for herself, which was not a rhythm at all but a way of disappearing. Scrub. Rinse. Wring. Hang. The motion was the same every day, and the sameness was the point. If you did the same thing in the same way at the same time, no one had to think about you, and if no one thought about you, no one had a reason to hurt you. She had been twelve when she worked that out. She had spent the eight years since proving it.

Her hands were the hands of someone older. The skin was cracked at the knuckles and white at the nails, and in winter the cracks opened and bled and closed again, and she had stopped noticing them the way you stop noticing a scar. She was tall enough to reach the high shelves and small enough to be ignored, which was the exact combination that kept her useful and invisible at the same time. She did not eat much. There was never quite enough, and she had learned early that hunger was a thing you could carry the way you carried the cold — as weather, not as complaint.

A floorboard creaked at the corridor mouth.

Leah's shoulders tightened before she told them to. She reached for the scrubbing brush, her fingers closing around the wet wood, and did not look up until she was certain the corridor was empty. She checked twice. First with her eyes. Then with her ears, tilting her head just enough to catch the faintest echo of a retreating step. She had learned to do this in her first month, when the other servants still thought she might be a threat, and she had kept doing it for eight years because the checking was the only thing that made the corridor feel like hers.

Empty.

She let out a breath she had been holding for eight years.

Cassian found her there.

He was fourteen, sharp-shouldered and sharp-tongued, wearing the king's colors because the queen allowed it. He filled the doorway the way a knife fills a wound — unnecessary, sharp, and intended. He had grown two inches since the spring and he had not yet decided what to do with the height, so he stood too close to doorways and too far from the people he was speaking to, as if distance were a kind of power.

"The northern lord's carriage leaves at dusk," he said. His voice had dropped an octave that morning and he was performing the new register badly, like a boy wearing his father's coat. "She says you'll be ready. No tricks, no hiding. You sign, or she makes you sign."

Leah did not answer. She dipped the brush and dragged it across the fabric in front of her in slow, even strokes. The lye made her eyes water. She did not wipe them.

"Did you hear me?" Cassian stepped closer. He stopped at the edge of the trough — close enough to be heard, far enough to claim he had never touched her. The queen was precise about that. No marks. No bruises. A princess who looked bruised was a martyr; a princess who looked willing was a political win.

"I heard," Leah said.

"Say it back."

"I heard."

Cassian smiled. It was not a kind smile. It was the smile of a boy who had been given a knife and told it was a sword. "You'll thank her one day. Lord Varnholt is rich. You'll have a roof. You won't smell like this." He looked at the trough, at her hands, at the gray water, with the delicate disgust of someone who had never scrubbed anything in his life. "You should be grateful. She could have married you to a pig farmer. She chose a lord."

Leah said nothing. She had learned, a long time ago, that nothing was the only answer that did not make things worse.

"Do you know what happens to girls who say no?" Cassian said. He was enjoying himself. "They disappear. Not dramatically. Not with a scandal. They just... stop being relevant. And then one day they're gone, and no one remembers them, and the castle goes on exactly as it did before." He leaned against the doorframe. "I'm not threatening you. I'm explaining how the world works. Mother says I explain things well."

He turned and left. Leah waited until his footsteps faded completely, then she set the brush down and pressed her wet palms against her apron. They shook. She held them there until they stopped.

The water in the trough turned gray with the dirt of people who did not know they were being cleaned.

## Scene 2

The queen found her an hour later.

Leah was still at the trough. She had not moved because there was nowhere to move to, and because moving without permission was a thing that got noticed. The lye had dried on her hands in a white film that cracked when she flexed her fingers.

Mirela was forty-plus, tall, greying auburn hair coiled tight, pale eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. She wore the rose-and-darker perfume she had worn since Leah was twelve. She did not smell like a mother. She smelled like decisions. She had come down the stairs without making a sound, which was its own kind of announcement, and she stood at the edge of the doorway the way she stood at the edge of everything — close enough to be obeyed, far enough to be untouchable.

"You have until dawn," the queen said.

She set the document on the edge of the trough. The paper was crisp. The seal was heavy. It sat there on the wet stone like something that had fallen from a great height and had not yet realized it was broken.

"Lord Varnholt has been patient," the queen said. "He will not be patient forever."

Leah did not touch the contract. She looked at the queen's hands instead — the long fingers, the ring that once belonged to the first queen, the one who died when Leah was twelve. She had worn that ring every day since. It looked wrong on her hand. It looked like something borrowed that had never been returned.

"May I ask why?" Leah said.

"Because you are a complication," the queen said. Her voice did not rise. It never rose. "Because Valdris needs a northern alliance. Because your father is gone and I am tired of looking at the daughter of the woman who took my place."

She said it the way she said everything — as if it were paperwork. As if Leah were a line item in a ledger that had finally been reviewed. There was no anger in it. That was the worst part. Anger would have meant Leah mattered enough to be angry at.

"The contract is generous," the queen said. "You will have a title. You will have protection. You will have a husband who is too old to be interested in you and too powerful to be refused. In exchange, you will sign, and you will leave, and you will not come back. That is the whole arrangement. There is nothing else to discuss."

Leah looked at the contract. The words were small and black. She could not read them through the water in her eyes.

"Do I have a choice?"

"You always have a choice. You just won't like the alternatives."

The queen turned to leave, and then she stopped. She did not look back. She stood in the doorway with her back to Leah, and for a moment she was only a shape against the light from the corridor, tall and still and perfectly composed.

"I did what was necessary," she said. "You will understand that one day, when you have something of your own to protect."

She left. Leah stood in the lye water until her legs ached.

Her legs ached. Her hands burned. The contract sat on the edge of the trough where the queen left it, and the seal caught the light from the corridor and held it, a small bright disc that looked like a coin someone had dropped and forgotten. Leah looked at it for a long time.

She had never been asked for anything in her life. She had been told, and she had obeyed, and the difference between those two things was the difference between a person and a thing. The queen had not asked. The queen had informed. And that, Leah thought, was the truest thing anyone had ever said to her about how the castle worked.

## Scene 3

She found the locket in the small room the queen had given her — a closet masquerading as a chamber, with a window that looked at a wall. The room had been a linen cupboard once. You could still see the nail holes where the shelves had been, and the faint rectangular shadow on the far wall where they had hung for fifty years before someone needed a place to put a princess who was no longer a princess.

The locket was broken. The chain had snapped years ago, or maybe it had been cut. The two halves sat on the windowsill like a pair of closed eyes. Leah sat on the edge of the bed and held the pieces together in her palm. The metal was cold. The picture inside was her mother, young and smiling, standing in the garden before the roses were cut down. Queen Elara. First wife. The only mother Leah had known.

She had been twelve when the fever came. Twelve when the queen coughed into her handkerchief and the court physician shook his head and said it was a wasting sickness. Leah had held her hand through the last night. The queen had been light as paper. She had been beautiful until she was not, and then she had been gone, and the funeral had been the kind of funeral that is arranged by the people who benefited from it.

Now Leah turned the locket over in her fingers and looked at the residue on the hinge — the brown crust that was not rust, not quite, and not anything she could name. She did not know what it was. She only knew that her mother had been healthy one week and gone the next, and that the queen had been sitting by the bed holding the queen's hand, and that the physician had been promoted to the northern provinces and never spoken of again.

She did not know. She only suspected. That was all she allowed herself to have. No notes. No letters. No proof. Just the weight of the locket and the smell of lye on her hands and the quiet understanding that the woman who ruled the castle had not done so by accident.

If she stayed, she would marry a man she had never met and she would disappear into the north and no one would ask what happened to her. If she ran, she would be a fugitive princess in a kingdom that wanted her married, and the queen's men would look for her, and they would find her, because there was nowhere to go that was not somewhere.

The room was cold. It was always cold. The window had been bricked up on the outside years ago — she could see the edge of the brick through the gap where the frame did not quite meet the wall — and the only air that came in came from the corridor, which smelled of the kitchens and the laundry and the thousand small fires that kept the castle alive. She had slept in this room for eight years. She had measured it in the dark so many times that she could cross it with her eyes closed, and she had, more than once, on the nights when the corridor was not safe.

She put the locket in her pocket. The metal pressed cold against her thigh through the thin fabric of her skirt.

Dawn was hours away. She had time.

She did not sleep. She sat on the edge of the bed and watched the wall opposite and listened to the castle breathe — the creak of timber, the distant cough of a guard, the whisper of a candle guttering in a sconce. Eight years of listening. She knew which stairs groaned. She knew which passages the servants used after midnight. She knew the route from the kitchens to the postern gate that the queen ordered bricked up three years ago, because the queen feared things coming in and things getting out, and Leah had pried the loose stone free one afternoon when she was sixteen and kept the memory of the gap ever since.

She had never used it. She had never had anywhere to go. Now she had nowhere to stay.

She stood up. She wore the plain gray dress of the servants. Her feet were bare under the hem because her shoes had been taken for Cassian's page. She did not take anything else. No cloak. No food. No proof. She looked around the room that had been hers for eight years, and she did not feel anything about leaving it, which was itself a kind of grief.

She opened the door and checked the corridor. Empty. She stepped into it and closed the door behind her without a sound.

## Scene 4

The castle was a mouth. It swallowed sound.

Leah moved through its corridors with her back pressed to the wall, her bare feet making no noise on the cold stone. The torches had been doused an hour ago. The only light came from the high windows, thin blades of moonlight that fell across the floor in places and left the rest in black. She knew this route. She had traced it in her mind a hundred times, lying awake at night, planning an escape that was never more than a daydream because the world outside was a place she had never been alone in, and fear is a wall as high as any rampart.

She knew the third stair from the top creaked. She stepped over it. She knew the guard changed at the half hour, and that the new man always stopped at the kitchen door for a cup of wine, and that the old man always left the wine on the table and went to the privy first. She knew the exact seven seconds between the door closing and the footsteps returning. She had counted them.

She did not think about what would happen if she was caught. Thinking about it would have stopped her, and stopping was the same as dying, so she thought about the next step instead, and then the next, and then the next.

The air changed as she went down. It got colder, and damper, and it smelled less like torch smoke and more like earth. The walls here were older. The stone was rougher. This was the underpart of the castle, the part that had been built before the kingdom had a name, and it felt like the inside of a throat.

She heard voices.

She stopped. She pressed herself into an alcove that had once held a statue, and she made herself small, and she did not breathe. Two guards, walking the lower corridor. Their lantern light swept the floor in front of them, a moving bar of yellow that came closer and closer and then, at the last moment, turned away.

She waited until the light was gone. Then she moved again.

The postern gap was where she left it. She had checked it three times that week. The stone moved when she pushed it. She pulled it free slowly, the mortar crumbling in her palm, and knelt in the opening. The night air hit her like a slap. It was autumn. The breath came out in white ribbons.

Beyond the gap was a ten-foot drop into the alley.

She climbed through. Her fingers found the ledge on the far side. She hung for a moment by her hands, looking down at the dark, and then she let go because there was nothing else to do.

The landing jarred her teeth. She rolled, instinct and eight years of falling, and came up against the wet cobbles of the alley. Her knees bled. Her palms were scraped raw. She did not stop. She pushed up and ran.

The streets of the lower city were narrow and dark and smelled of coal smoke and something sour she could not name. She had never been here alone. She had never been here at all. The castle loomed behind her, huge and black and false, and she ran away from it with her hands in her pockets and the locket pressed hard against her leg.

A patrol passed.

She pressed into the shadow of a doorway and held her breath. Her heart was beating so hard she was certain they could hear it. The boots clacked on the wet stones. The voices were lazy, bored, the voices of men who had walked this beat a hundred times and found nothing and expected to find nothing. They did not see her. They were looking for a princess in silk. They were not looking for a servant girl with raw hands and a gray dress.

She waited until they were gone. Then she ran again.

She ran until the street widened and the houses stopped being houses and became warehouses, and then she slowed because her body would not run anymore. She walked. She kept her head down. She kept her hands in her pockets where the cold could not find them. A dog barked somewhere and she flinched so hard she nearly fell. A cart passed her, the driver half-asleep, and she pressed herself against a wall until it was gone. She did not know where she was going. She knew only that the direction was away, and that away was the only direction that mattered.

She ran until her lungs burned. She ran until the stone turned to packed earth and the packed earth turned to mud. She ran until the castle lights were gone and the only light came from shop windows that showed rooms full of warmth and food and people who did not know her name. Her feet were bleeding. Her side ached. She did not stop, because stopping meant being eaten.

She did not feel like a princess. She felt like an animal that had broken its leg and was still running.

## Scene 5

The Red Velvet Inn appeared like a wound in the street — deep red velvet hung above the door, gilded and heavy, and the light that leaked around it was gold and warm and smelled of meat and wine and smoke. Music thudded through the wood. Laughter. A woman's bright voice singing something in a language Leah did not know.

She had never heard music from the outside. Not really. The castle had concerts, but they were formal and distant, and she had been made to sit at the back where no one would look at her. This was different. This was alive. This was a sound that did not care who she was.

Leah reached the door. She could not feel her feet. Her hands were numb. Her throat was too tight to swallow. She stood on the step for a moment and looked at the velvet, at the gold, at the light, and she thought: if I go in there, I am not a princess anymore. I am whatever they decide I am.

She pushed the velvet aside and stepped into the noise.

The room was vast and warm and full of faces. A bar ran along one wall. Girls in silk sat on men's laps. The air was thick with perfume and smoke and the clink of glass. Leah stood in the doorway with wet mud on her dress and her breath coming in ragged gasps and her heart beating so hard she thought it might break her ribs. She had run across a city to get here, and now that she was here she did not know what she was supposed to do next.

A man looked at her. Then looked away. A girl laughed at something someone had said. The warmth hit her face like a hand, and she realized she had not been warm in hours, and that her teeth were chattering, and that there was mud on her dress and blood on her feet and she was standing in the doorway of a brothel with the face of a girl who had just fallen off a wall. No one screamed. No one pointed. This was a place where people arrived in worse condition than hers, and the room simply absorbed her the way it absorbed everything.

A woman looked up from behind the bar.

She was in her forties, with mahogany hair swept to one side and amber eyes that moved like mercury. She set a glass down with a soft click and studied Leah for one second — just one — and then she smiled. It was not the smile of someone who was surprised. It was the smile of someone who had been expecting something and had finally received it.

"Little bird," the woman said, "you look like you've been running from wolves."

Leah could not answer. She could only breathe.

"Elowen," the woman said. She nodded at the staircase. "Upstairs, second door on the left. Clean yourself. I'll send someone with water and food. You don't need to speak."

Leah took one step. Then another. The floor did not creak. The noise of the room softened around her like water closing over a stone. She was inside. She was out of the cold. She was alive.

Behind her, a merchant with ink on his fingers leaned toward Elowen and said something low about a host in the east who was gathering an army the size of a myth.

Elowen did not look at him. She watched Leah climb the stairs, one careful step at a time, and her amber eyes held the particular warmth of a person who had just found something valuable and had not yet decided how much it would cost.

## Chapter 2

## Chapter 2: The Lower City

## Scene 1

The inn smelled of cedar smoke and money.

Leah had been given a room on the third floor—small, clean, with a lock on the door and a window that looked out over a courtyard where laundry hung like pale flags. She had slept for eleven hours. She woke with the sun on her face and the taste of wine still on her tongue, and for a moment she did not know where she was. Then the memories came back: the flight, the wall, the streets, the velvet door, Elowen's amber eyes.

She was still alive. That was the first thing.

The second thing was that she was hungry.

She washed her face in the basin left by Elowen's maid. She changed into the plain dress that had been laid out for her—grey wool, simple, the kind a servant would wear. She braided her hair with fingers that had grown steadier overnight. She looked at herself in the small mirror and saw a girl who was not a princess, not a servant, not anything in between. A girl who was, for the first time in eight years, unclaimed.

She went downstairs.

The main room was quiet in the morning. A few girls were cleaning, sweeping the floor, wiping down the bar. They moved with the unhurried rhythm of people who worked this floor like breathing. They looked at Leah with frank curiosity—she was new, she was young, she had the kind of face that made people look twice. Leah kept her head down and found a seat near the window.

The room was built like a trap and decorated like a promise. Low ceilings, thick curtains, chairs arranged in small groups that invited intimacy without demanding it. The light was dim even at midday, filtered through coloured glass and heavy drapes. It was a place designed for secrets, and it wore that purpose comfortably. Leah had spent eight years in a castle designed for power. This was the same architecture, inverted—instead of making people feel small, it made them feel safe. Safe enough to say the thing they could not say at home.

She watched a girl cross the room with a tray of glasses. The girl moved the way the senior servants had moved in the castle—economical, deliberate, eyes down. Leah recognised the posture. She had worn it herself.

Elowen found her there an hour later.

"Little bird," she said, sliding into the seat across from her. "You slept well."

"I slept," Leah said.

"Good. You'll need your strength." Elowen set a cup of tea in front of her. Real tea, not the weak stuff the servants drank. "We need to talk about the terms."

Leah wrapped her hands around the cup. The warmth steadied her. She had learned, in the castle, that warmth was always offered for a reason, and that the reason was never free.

"You're not a servant here," Elowen said. "You're a commodity. That sounds cruel, but it's honest, and honesty is what keeps you alive in this house. You have something the clients want—youth, beauty, a certain quality that makes men feel like they're the first. You'll keep that quality as long as you follow my rules."

Leah nodded.

"Rule one," Elowen said. "Young clients only. No one over thirty. No one who drinks to excess. No one with a reputation for cruelty. I have a list. If a name isn't on it, the answer is no. You don't negotiate. You don't make exceptions. You tell them to come back when they've sobered up."

She said it without apology. This was not a negotiation. It was a condition of employment, delivered the way a merchant might quote a price.

"Rule two. Discretion. These men have wives, families, positions. What happens in the Red Velvet Inn stays in the Red Velvet Inn. You never use their real names. You never tell them yours. You never, ever repeat what you hear."

Leah looked up. "What I hear?"

Elowen smiled. It was a merchant's smile, warm and sharp at the edges. "Rule three. Information is worth more than flesh. These men talk. They talk about their wives, their debts, their business partners, their politics. You listen. You remember. You tell me."

"And what do you do with it?"

"I trade it. A lord's debt becomes leverage. A merchant's secret becomes a favor. An officer's complaint becomes a warning. In this house, secrets are currency, and you're about to become very rich."

Leah sat with that for a moment. She had spent eight years learning to be invisible, to hear without being heard, to remember without being noticed. She had thought those were survival skills. Elowen was telling her they were marketable.

"What do you take?" Leah asked.

"Half. Half of everything you earn, half of everything I learn from what you tell me. You keep the other half. You save it. You build yourself a future." Elowen leaned forward. "I'm not your enemy, little bird. I'm your business partner. The better you do, the better I do. That's the only arrangement that lasts."

Leah looked at her. She had never had a business partner before. She had never had an arrangement that lasted. In the castle, everything had been held together by fear—the queen's fear of martyrs, the servants' fear of starvation, Leah's fear of everything.

"And if I refuse?" Leah said.

Elowen's amber eyes did not move. "Then you leave. I don't keep birds in cages. But you'll leave with nothing, and the lower city is not kind to girls with nothing. So think carefully before you answer."

Leah thought about the postern gap. She thought about the ten-foot drop into the alley. She thought about the cold and the hunger and the patrols. She thought about the castle, which was not an option, and the road north, which was not survivable, and the east, which was a rumour.

"I accept," she said.

## Scene 2

The first client came that evening.

His name was not his name—he gave a false one, something common, something that would not be remembered. He was young, maybe twenty-five, with soft hands and nervous eyes. He had the look of a merchant's son, someone spending money he had not earned on something he had been taught to feel guilty about.

Leah met him in the suite on the second floor. The older girl had dressed her—silk, not too much, hair loose. The older girl had told her what to do. Leah knew the routine. She had learned it in the castle, in the dark, in the rooms where no one asked her name.

But this was different. This was a transaction. She had said yes.

He was clumsy. He was in a hurry. He did not ask her name, did not ask if she was comfortable, did not ask anything at all. He moved through it like a man crossing a room—quick, efficient, focused on the far wall. He finished fast. He paid. He left.

Leah lay on the bed for a moment afterward, staring at the ceiling. She felt nothing. Not pleasure, not pain, not even the dull ache of endurance. She felt like a room someone had walked through.

She got up. She washed. She went downstairs.

Elowen was waiting.

"Well?" she said.

"It was fine," Leah said.

"Fine," Elowen repeated. She laughed, a short, knowing sound. "You'll learn. The first one is always fine. It's the ones who come after that teach you what you're worth."

Leah sat down across from her. "The second rule. Discretion. You said I never repeat what I hear."

"That's right."

"But you also said information is worth more than flesh. So which is it—do I keep secrets or do I sell them?"

Elowen's smile widened. "You sell them to me. Only to me. You never sell them to anyone else, and you never sell them outside this house. Inside these walls, you are a vault with a single key. I hold the key. You hold the secrets. That's the arrangement."

"And if a client tells me something about you?"

"Then you tell me that too." Elowen's eyes were bright. "I have no secrets from myself, little bird. But it's good that you asked. It means you're thinking like a merchant."

Leah thought about that. She had spent eight years thinking like a servant—quiet, obedient, invisible. Now she was being asked to think like a merchant. It felt like learning a new language, one where the words meant something different than they had always meant.

The second client came two nights later. He was not like the first. He was silent. He did not apologize. He did not talk. He moved with the focused economy of a man who had been here before and wanted it over with as little mess as possible. He did not look at her face. He did not ask her name. He finished, paid, and left without a word.

Leah lay on the bed afterward and thought about the difference. The first man had wanted permission. The second man had wanted nothing at all—not connection, not confession, not even the pretence of it. He had wanted a service, the way a man wants a shoeshine. And that, she realised, was its own kind of information. A man who wanted nothing was a man who had learned to want nothing, and a man who had learned to want nothing was dangerous in a way the nervous ones were not.

That night she lay awake and catalogued. The merchant's son. Nervous, guilty, hurried. The silent man. Efficient, empty, practised. She filed them both away. She did not know what use the files would be, but Bryn had said the vault mattered, and so she built it. Two files. Two types. Two kinds of men who paid for the same thing and wanted completely different things from it.

## Scene 3

The older girls ate together in the kitchen after midnight.

Leah had expected hostility—new girls were usually treated as competition, as threats, as reminders of what the older ones had been. But the girls of the Red Velvet Inn were not hostile. They were practical. They had a system, and Leah was now part of it.

There were four of them. Bryn, who had been there six years and had the calm, measuring eyes of a woman who had seen everything twice. Maren, who was quick and sharp and laughed at her own jokes. Sable, who rarely spoke but noticed everything. And Tess, who was youngest after Leah and still had the hopeful look of someone who believed the work would get easier.

They sat around the table with bread and cheese and weak beer, and they talked. Not about the clients—they had rules about that—but about the house, the rhythms, the patterns.

"Listen," Bryn said. She was talking to Leah, her voice low and steady. "The men who come here aren't paying for sex. They're paying for permission. Permission to say things they can't say at home. Permission to be weak. Permission to be whoever they are when no one's watching."

"So I'm a priest," Leah said.

"You're a vault," Bryn said. "They put their secrets in you and they walk out lighter. Your job is to keep the vault locked."

"Until Elowen asks you to open it," Maren added.

"Until Elowen asks you to open it," Bryn agreed. "But here's the thing—you don't have to wait for her to ask. You can listen for yourself. You can remember what matters. You can build your own vault, and one day you'll have something worth more than gold."

Leah thought about that. She had spent eight years in a castle full of secrets. She had heard the queen's private conversations, Cassian's whispered cruelties, the servants' gossip about who was sleeping with whom and who owed money to whom. She had never thought of it as valuable. She had thought of it as survival.

"What kind of secrets?" she said.

"Everything," Sable said. It was the first time she had spoken all evening. "Who's betraying whom. Who's running out of money. Who's planning to leave. Who's planning to stay. Who's loyal and who's just waiting for the right price."

"And the right price is always information," Bryn said. "Gold runs out. Land can be taken. But knowing what someone did at midnight in a room they thought was secret—that's power. That's the kind of power that doesn't disappear when the money's gone."

She broke off a piece of bread and ate it slowly, giving Leah time to absorb.

"Let me give you an example," Bryn said. "Three months ago a lord came in. Drunk, angry, talking about his brother. His brother had the inheritance, the title, the land. He had nothing but a debt and a grudge. He said things he shouldn't have said. He said his brother was not his father's son. He said he had proof."

Leah looked up.

"The next week," Bryn said, "his brother paid a visit. Not to me. To his brother's creditors. Within a month the debt was called in, the brother was ruined, and the lord who had nothing now had everything. That's what information does. It doesn't just win arguments. It moves money. It moves land. It moves power."

"And who told you that?" Leah said.

"The brother's wife." Bryn smiled. "Who was tired of her husband's debts and saw an opportunity. She told me, I told the lord, the lord moved. Everyone got what they wanted. Except the brother, who lost everything and never knew why."

Leah sat with that. It was a small story. It was also a map of how the world worked when you had the right information at the right time.

"Remember the officer who came in last week?" Bryn said. "The one who talked about the east?"

Leah nodded. She had heard him. Everyone had heard him.

"He'll be back," Bryn said. "Men who talk once talk again. And next time, he'll say more. That's the pattern. First they test the water. Then they dive in. Your job is to be the water."

Leah looked around the table. These women were not ladies-in-waiting. They were not servants. They were something else—something the castle had no name for. They were survivors who had learned to turn survival into skill.

"There's one more thing," Tess said. She had been quiet all evening, and when she spoke the others turned to listen. "The work is not the hard part. The hard part is the pretending. You have to pretend you're happy to be there. You have to pretend you like them. You have to pretend it doesn't matter. And the pretending eats you, a little at a time, until one day you wake up and you can't remember what you actually feel about anything."

"So what do you do?" Leah said.

"You keep one thing real," Tess said. "One thing that's yours and nobody else gets to touch. For me it's the coin. I count it every night. I know exactly how much I have. No one can pretend that away."

Leah thought about that. She had the locket in her pocket. It was the only thing she had carried from the castle that was hers. Maybe that was her one real thing.

"I want to learn," Leah said.

Bryn smiled. "Good. Because the world is changing, and the women who know things will be the ones who survive it."

## Scene 4

The officer came in drunk on the third night.

Leah was in the main room, sitting with a merchant who was telling her about his shipments—ostriches, he said, from the southern provinces, very rare, very expensive. She was listening. She was remembering. She was doing what Bryn had taught her. The merchant was a talker. He told her about his routes, his suppliers, the prices he paid and the prices he charged. She filed it all away. She did not know what use the file would be, but she was learning that the use often came later, when you had enough files to see the pattern.

The officer was young, maybe thirty, in the uniform of the city guard. He was loud. He was drunk. He was talking about the east.

"—a host," he said. "A host in the east. An army. They say it's the size of a myth. They say a woman leads them. They say they're coming this way."

The room went quiet. Not the silence of attention—the silence of people who had heard something they did not want to hear.

"Officer," Elowen said from behind the bar. Her voice was smooth, warm, the voice of a woman who had heard everything and was surprised by nothing. "Perhaps you'd like some water."

"I'm telling you," the officer said. "They're gathering. They've been gathering for months. The border lords are worried. The queen's council is worried. Everyone's worried, and no one's saying it out loud because saying it out loud makes it real."

"And what do they say in the east?" Elowen said. She was moving closer now, her amber eyes fixed on the officer's face.

"They say there's an empire. A woman runs it. She's got an army of women—girls, even—raised from childhood to fight. They say she's conquered everything east of the mountains and now she's looking west. They say she's coming for us."

"Coming for us," Elowen repeated. "That's a big claim."

"It's not a claim. It's a fact. The border guards have seen the dust clouds. The traders have seen the roads filling up with wagons. Something is moving in the east, and it's moving this way."

He drank his water. He left. The room returned to its usual hum—laughter, conversation, the clink of glasses. But something had changed. Leah could feel it. The air was tighter. The laughter was a little too loud.

Elowen came to Leah's table. She sat down. She did not speak for a long moment.

"You heard that," she said.

"I heard it."

"What do you think?"

Leah thought about the castle. She thought about the queen, who had married her father after the first queen died—suddenly, in her bed, when Leah was twelve—and then sent her father to die on the front. She thought about Cassian, who had been taught to hate her. She thought about the servants' wing, the lye, the cold stone, the eight years of being no one.

"I think," Leah said slowly, "that the east is very far away, and the castle is very close, and I don't know which one is more dangerous."

Elowen smiled. It was not a merchant's smile this time. It was something sharper, something almost like respect.

"You're learning," she said.

Leah did not answer. She was thinking about the officer's words. A host in the east. A woman who led them. An army that had conquered everything east of the mountains and was now looking west. It was a rumour. Rumours were currency too, she realised. The officer had not known he was selling anything, but he had. And Elowen had bought it, and now Leah had it too.

She filed it away. She did not know what use it would be. But she was learning that the world was not made of castles and brothels and roads. It was made of information moving from one person to another, and the people who moved it were the ones who survived.

She finished her tea. She went upstairs. She braided her hair. She looked at herself in the mirror and saw a girl who was learning to listen.
